Australia's Animal Protection Areas Distribution

Introduction

Australia has a large and diverse protected-area network, including national parks, nature reserves, Indigenous Protected Areas, conservation reserves and marine protected areas. These places are important because they protect habitats, support biodiversity conservation and form the spatial foundation of Australia's animal protection system.

However, the existence of many protected areas does not automatically mean that animal conservation needs are evenly covered. Some regions contain many small protected areas near cities and coastlines, while remote inland regions contain fewer but much larger reserves. This means that a national total can hide very different local patterns of protection.

This visualisation follows a three-part story. First, it examines where protected areas are located and how their size varies across Australia. Second, it compares the types and management roles of protected areas. Finally, it connects these patterns with threatened animal data to ask the central question: are protected areas located where animal protection needs are greatest?

How to Read

From Protected Areas to Animal Protection Need

The visualisation is organised as a guided story. It begins with the national spatial pattern of protected areas, then compares protected-area categories and growth over time, and finally connects these patterns with threatened animal data. Larger map symbols show protected-area size, darker map colours show higher coverage or density, and the final charts ask whether protected land appears to align with animal conservation need.

Domain: Australian biodiversity conservation. Audience: a general public and university audience interested in environmental policy, protected areas and threatened animal protection.

EPBC status describes the conservation threat category of species or ecological communities under Australian environmental law.

IUCN category describes the management purpose of a protected area, such as national park, habitat management or sustainable use.

Gazettal year means the year a protected area was officially recorded or declared.

Chapter 1

Where Are Australia's Protected Areas?

The first part of the story examines spatial distribution. Protected areas are not spread evenly across the country: some regions contain many small sites, while others contain fewer but much larger reserves. These maps and state comparisons show how location and area size shape the national pattern.

1. Major Protected Areas Across Australia

This proportional symbol map focuses on five major protected-area categories and only includes sites of 50,000 hectares or larger. Filtering the data reduces visual clutter from thousands of smaller sites while keeping the protected areas that contribute most to national land protection. Circle size represents area, so larger symbols indicate larger protected areas.

Inland Australia contributes much of the protected-area total through a smaller number of very large reserves, while coastal Australia has many smaller protected areas. This means the national protected-area network is shaped by two different patterns: large remote reserves in the interior and smaller, denser protected sites near the coast.

2. Protected Land Is Concentrated in a Few States

This bar chart compares total protected-area hectares by state and territory. It focuses on absolute protected area, showing which states contribute the largest amount of land to the national protected-area network.

Western Australia, the Northern Territory and South Australia dominate the national total. This reflects both their large land areas and the presence of very large inland protected areas. However, absolute area alone does not show how much of each state is protected.

3. State Coverage Is More Meaningful After Normalisation

Instead of only comparing raw protected-area hectares, this choropleth map compares protected area as a percentage of each state's land area. This normalised view creates a fairer comparison between large and small states.

Normalising by state land area changes the interpretation. A large state does not automatically appear important just because it has more land. The map instead highlights where protection covers a larger share of the state.

Chapter 2

Which Protected-Area Types Dominate?

The second part examines the structure of Australia's protected-area network. Here, "type" refers to categories of protected areas, such as Indigenous Protected Areas, national parks, nature reserves and conservation reserves. These categories contribute differently: some contain many small sites, while others cover very large areas.

4. Protected-Area Types Contribute Unequally

This labelled bar chart compares how much protected land each protected-area type contributes to the national network. A bar chart is used instead of a donut chart because it makes category comparison clearer and allows the exact percentage values to be labelled.

Indigenous Protected Areas contribute the largest share of protected land by area. This shows that Australia's protected-area network is shaped not only by national parks, but also by Indigenous land management and other reserve categories.

5. The Largest Protected Areas Are Mostly Inland

This ranking shows the largest individual protected areas. It identifies the specific places that contribute most strongly to Australia's total protected-area hectares.

The largest protected areas are mostly inland and remote. This explains why the national protected-area total can be strongly influenced by a relatively small number of very large sites.

6. Protection Has Expanded Over Time

The cumulative area chart shows when protected areas entered the network based on available gazettal years. The small chart below acts as an interactive time selector: drag across it to zoom into a specific period.

Protected-area growth does not increase evenly. The steep rises suggest periods when major new protected areas were added, rather than slow and constant growth over time. Use the lower mini chart to focus on specific decades and inspect when the largest jumps occurred.

Chapter 3

Are Protected Areas Protecting Animals?

The final part shifts from protected-area distribution to animal conservation need. A state can have a large protected-area network, but that does not automatically mean it has fewer threatened animals or that protection is located where animal pressure is highest. These charts compare threatened animal species, animal groups and EPBC threat status across states.

7. Animal Protection Need Does Not Match Land Coverage

This quadrant scatter plot compares protected-area coverage with animal conservation need. Each point represents one state or territory. The dashed vertical line shows the median protected-area coverage, while the dashed horizontal line shows the median number of threatened animal species. This divides states into four groups and helps identify places where animal need is high but protected land coverage is relatively low.

Key insight: New South Wales and Queensland sit in the high-need and lower-coverage area. They have many threatened animal species, but their protected-area coverage is not as high as some smaller states. This suggests that protected-area extent alone does not fully explain animal protection need.

8. Animal Groups Face Different Levels of Threat

This bubble chart compares listed animal groups using three measures at once. The horizontal axis shows the total number of listed animal species in each group, the vertical axis shows the number of critically endangered and endangered species, and bubble size represents the number of vulnerable species. This makes it possible to compare both the scale and severity of animal threat.

Key insight: birds and mammals contain many listed species, but reptiles and fish also show substantial high-risk listings. This means animal protection cannot be treated as one single problem. Different animal groups depend on different habitats, so protected areas may support some groups more effectively than others.

9. Threatened Animal Groups Are Uneven Across States

This heatmap compares threatened animal groups across states and territories. Each cell represents one animal group in one state, and darker colour indicates a higher number of listed threatened animal species. The chart helps show whether animal conservation pressure is concentrated in particular states or spread across multiple animal groups.

Key insight: animal threat patterns are geographically uneven. New South Wales and Queensland stand out across several animal groups, while some smaller states show more concentrated patterns. This helps show that conservation gaps are not only about how much land is protected, but also about which animal groups are under pressure in each state.

10. Threat Severity Also Differs by State

This stacked percentage chart compares the EPBC threat-status composition of listed animals in each state and territory. Instead of only counting how many threatened animals each state has, it shows whether the listed animals are mainly vulnerable, endangered or critically endangered. This helps separate the amount of animal pressure from the severity of that pressure.

Key insight: states differ not only in the number of threatened animals, but also in the seriousness of those listings. A state with fewer listed animals may still have a high share of endangered or critically endangered species. This reinforces the main argument: protection should be evaluated against animal conservation need, not only against protected-area size.

Conclusion

Protected Area Size Alone Does Not Explain Animal Protection

Overall, Australia's protected-area network is extensive, but its distribution is uneven. Large inland reserves dominate the total protected area, while coastal and south-eastern regions contain many smaller protected areas. This means that protected-area size, protected-area density and geographic location must be interpreted together rather than treated as one simple national total.

The visualisations also show that protected-area type matters. Indigenous Protected Areas and national parks contribute strongly to total protected land, while other categories play different management roles or contain many smaller sites. The network is therefore not just a map of land coverage; it is also a system of different protection purposes, histories and management categories.

When the protected-area pattern is compared with threatened animal data, the key message becomes clearer: more protected land does not always mean lower animal conservation pressure. Some states have many threatened animal species despite relatively lower protected-area coverage, while other states have higher coverage but fewer threatened animal species. This suggests that conservation planning should consider both the amount of protected land and whether that land aligns with animal habitats and threat patterns.

The main conclusion is that Australia's protected-area network is large, but animal protection is still uneven. A stronger conservation strategy should not only ask “how much land is protected?”, but also “where is protection located, what type of protection is it, and does it match the animals most in need?”
