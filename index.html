Australia's Animal Protection Areas Distribution

Introduction

Australia has a large and diverse protected-area network, including national parks, nature reserves, Indigenous Protected Areas, conservation reserves and marine protected areas. These places are important because they protect habitats, support biodiversity conservation and form the spatial foundation of Australia's animal protection system.

However, the existence of many protected areas does not automatically mean that animal conservation needs are evenly covered. Some regions contain many small protected areas near cities and coastlines, while remote inland regions contain fewer but much larger reserves. This means that a national total can hide very different local patterns of protection.

This visualisation follows a three-part story. First, it examines where protected areas are located and how their size varies across Australia. Second, it compares the types and management roles of protected areas. Finally, it connects these patterns with threatened animal data to ask the central question: are protected areas located where animal protection needs are greatest?

How to Read

From Protected Areas to Animal Protection Need

The visualisation is organised as a guided story. It begins with the national spatial pattern of protected areas, then compares protected-area categories and growth over time, and finally connects these patterns with threatened animal data. Larger map symbols show protected-area size, darker map colours show higher coverage or density, and the final charts ask whether protected land appears to align with animal conservation need.

Domain: Australian biodiversity conservation. Audience: a general public and university audience interested in environmental policy, protected areas and threatened animal protection.

Australian threatened species status describes the conservation threat category of species or ecological communities under Australian environmental law.

Protected-area management category describes the management purpose of a protected area, such as national park, habitat management or sustainable use.

Gazettal year means the year a protected area was officially recorded or declared.

Chapter 1

Where Are Australia's Protected Areas?

The first part of the story examines spatial distribution. Protected areas are not spread evenly across the country: some regions contain many small sites, while others contain fewer but much larger reserves. These maps and state comparisons show how location and area size shape the national pattern.

1. Major Protected Areas Across Australia

This proportional symbol map focuses on five major protected-area categories and only includes sites of 50,000 hectares or larger. Filtering the data reduces visual clutter from thousands of smaller sites while keeping the protected areas that contribute most to national land protection. Circle size is proportional to protected-area hectares, so larger circles represent larger protected areasCircle colour shows the protected-area type; hover over any circle to see its name, state and International Union for Conservation of Nature management category

Inland Australia contributes a large share of protected land through fewer but much larger reserves. Coastal Australia has many protected areas, but most are smaller. This means Australia's protected-area system is spatially uneven: land protection is strong in some remote regions, while animal pressure may still remain high in more populated coastal areas.

2. Protected Land Is Concentrated in a Few States

This bar chart compares the total amount of protected land in each state and territory. It shows which parts of Australia contribute most to the national protected-area total. This is useful for understanding overall scale, but it does not yet show whether protection is proportional to each state's land size or whether it matches animal conservation need.

Western Australia, the Northern Territory and South Australia dominate the national total. This reflects both their large land areas and the presence of very large inland protected areas. However, absolute area alone does not show how much of each state is protected.

3. State Coverage Is More Meaningful After Normalisation

This choropleth map changes the comparison from total protected land to protected land as a percentage of each state or territory. This makes the comparison fairer because large states no longer appear important simply because they have more land. The map shows where protection covers a larger share of the state.

Normalising by land area changes the story. Some smaller places appear more strongly protected once their size is considered, while some large states look less dominant than in the total-area chart. This shows why protected-area distribution needs to be read in both absolute and proportional terms.

Chapter 2

Which Protected-Area Types Dominate?

The second part examines the structure of Australia's protected-area network. Here, "type" refers to categories of protected areas, such as Indigenous Protected Areas, national parks, nature reserves and conservation reserves. These categories contribute differently: some contain many small sites, while others cover very large areas.

4. Protected-Area Types Contribute Unequally

This labelled bar chart compares each protected-area type's share of Australia's total protected-area hectares. The horizontal axis shows the percentage contribution to the national protected-area network, and this bar chart compares each protected-area type's share of Australia's total protected-area hectares.This makes it clear which protection types contribute the most land area.

Indigenous Protected Areas contribute the largest share of protected land. This is important because Australia's protection system is not only built through national parks. It also depends heavily on Indigenous land management and other reserve systems, which play a major role in protecting large inland landscapes.

5. The Largest Protected Areas Are Mostly Inland

This ranking identifies the largest individual protected areas in Australia. Instead of showing all protected areas, it focuses on the sites that contribute most strongly to the national total. This helps explain why a small number of very large reserves can strongly shape Australia's overall protected-area statistics.

The largest protected areas are mostly inland and remote. This reinforces the pattern seen in the map: Australia's protected-area network is strongly influenced by large interior reserves. These sites increase the national protected-area total, but they do not necessarily represent protection in every habitat or animal-risk region.

6. Protection Has Expanded Over Time

The cumulative area chart shows when protected areas entered the network based on available gazettal years. The small chart below acts as an interactive time selector: drag across it to zoom into a specific period.

Protected-area growth does not rise evenly. The sharp increases suggest periods when large protected areas were added to the network, rather than slow and constant expansion. This matters because today's distribution is partly the result of historical decisions about when and where protection was declared.

Chapter 3

Are Protected Areas Protecting Animals?

The final part shifts from protected-area distribution to animal conservation need. A state can have a large protected-area network, but that does not automatically mean it has fewer threatened animals or that protection is located where animal pressure is highest. These charts compare threatened animal species, animal groups and EPBC threat status across states.

7. Animal Protection Need Does Not Match Land Coverage

This quadrant scatter plot compares protected-area coverage with animal conservation need. Each point represents one state or territory. The dashed vertical line shows the median protected-area coverage, while the dashed horizontal line shows the median number of threatened animal species. This divides states into four groups and helps identify places where animal need is high but protected land coverage is relatively low.

New South Wales and Queensland sit in the high-need and lower-coverage area. They have many threatened animal species, but their protected-area coverage is not as high as some smaller states. This suggests that protected-area extent alone does not fully explain animal protection need.Protected land is important, but the location and ecological relevance of that land matter just as much.

8. Animal Groups Face Different Levels of Threat

This bubble chart compares listed animal groups using three measures at once. The horizontal axis shows the total number of listed animal species in each group, the vertical axis shows the number of critically endangered and endangered species, and bubble size represents the number of vulnerable species. This makes it possible to compare both the scale and severity of animal threat.

Birds and mammals contain many listed species, but reptiles and fish also show substantial high-risk listings. This means animal protection cannot be treated as one single problem. Different animal groups depend on different habitats, so protected areas may support some groups more effectively than others.

9. Threatened Animal Groups Are Uneven Across States

This heatmap compares threatened animal groups across states and territories. Each cell represents one animal group in one state, and darker colour indicates a higher number of listed threatened animal species. The chart helps show whether animal conservation pressure is concentrated in particular states or spread across multiple animal groups.

Animal threat patterns are geographically uneven. New South Wales and Queensland stand out across several animal groups, while some smaller states show more concentrated patterns. This helps show that conservation gaps are not only about how much land is protected, but also about which animal groups are under pressure in each state.

10. Threat Severity Also Differs by State

This stacked percentage chart compares the Australian threatened species status composition of listed animals in each state and territory. Instead of only counting how many threatened animals each state has, it shows whether the listed animals are mainly vulnerable, endangered or critically endangered. This helps separate the amount of animal pressure from the severity of that pressure.

States differ not only in the number of threatened animals, but also in the seriousness of those listings. A state with fewer listed animals may still have a high share of endangered or critically endangered species. This reinforces the main argument: protection should be evaluated against animal conservation need, not only against protected-area size.

Conclusion

Protected Area Size Alone Does Not Explain Animal Protection

This visualisation shows that Australia's protected-area network is large, but its benefits are not evenly distributed. The national pattern is shaped by two very different forms of protection: large inland reserves that contribute most of the total protected area, and many smaller coastal protected areas that appear in denser clusters. This means the total amount of protected land can hide important regional differences.

The structure of the network also matters. Indigenous Protected Areas and national parks contribute strongly to protected land, while other categories play smaller but still important roles. These differences show that protected areas should not be read only as land coverage. They also represent different management purposes, histories and conservation priorities.

When the protected-area pattern is compared with threatened animal data, the central issue becomes clearer: more protected land does not automatically mean lower animal conservation need. Some states have many threatened animal species even though their protected-area coverage is not especially high. Other states protect a larger share of land, but have fewer threatened animal species. This suggests that area size alone is not enough to judge whether animals are being protected effectively.

The main finding is that Australia's protected-area system needs to be evaluated through both land and animals. A stronger conservation strategy should ask not only how much land is protected, but also where that protection is located, what type of protection it provides, and whether it overlaps with the habitats and species groups under the greatest pressure.
