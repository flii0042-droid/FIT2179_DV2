Australia's Animal Protection Areas Distribution

Introduction

Australia has one of the world's largest protected-area networks, covering national parks, nature reserves, Indigenous Protected Areas, conservation reserves and marine protected areas. These areas are central to biodiversity conservation because they help protect habitats, ecological communities and species records from land-use pressure.

However, protection is not evenly distributed. Some regions contain many small protected areas clustered near cities and coastlines, while remote inland regions contain fewer but much larger reserves. This means that the same national total can hide very different local patterns of protection.

This visualisation investigates how Australia's protected areas are distributed across space, state, protected-area type and management category. It then connects these patterns with species records and threatened ecological communities to ask a broader question: where does protection appear strong, and where might conservation gaps remain?

How to Read

From National Pattern to Conservation Gaps

The visualisation is organised as a guided story. It begins with the national spatial pattern of protected areas, then compares protected-area categories and growth over time, and finally connects these patterns with species records and threatened ecological communities. Larger symbols show area, darker map cells show density, and each insight box highlights the main takeaway.

Domain: Australian biodiversity conservation. Audience: a general public and university audience interested in environmental policy, protected areas and species protection.

EPBC status describes the conservation threat category of species or ecological communities under Australian environmental law.

IUCN category describes the management purpose of a protected area, such as national park, habitat management or sustainable use.

Gazettal year means the year a protected area was officially recorded or declared.

Chapter 1

Where Are Australia's Protected Areas?

The first part of the story examines spatial distribution. Protected areas are not spread evenly across the country: some regions contain many small sites, while others contain fewer but much larger reserves. These maps and state comparisons show how location and area size shape the national pattern.

1. Major Protected Areas Across Australia

This proportional symbol map focuses on five major protected-area categories and only includes sites of 50,000 hectares or larger. Filtering the data reduces visual clutter from thousands of smaller sites while keeping the protected areas that contribute most to national land protection. Circle size represents area, so larger symbols indicate larger protected areas.

Key insight: large protected areas are concentrated in inland and northern Australia. Coastal regions still contain many protected areas, but many are smaller and overlap heavily when every record is shown. This map is therefore a simplified national overview, not a complete inventory of every protected area.

2. Protected Land Is Concentrated in a Few States

This bar chart compares total protected-area hectares by state and territory. It focuses on absolute protected area, showing which states contribute the largest amount of land to the national protected-area network.

Key insight: Western Australia, the Northern Territory and South Australia dominate the national total. This reflects both their large land areas and the presence of very large inland protected areas. However, absolute area alone does not show how much of each state is protected.

3. State Coverage Is More Meaningful After Normalisation

Instead of only comparing raw protected-area hectares, this choropleth map compares protected area as a percentage of each state's land area. This normalised view creates a fairer comparison between large and small states.

Key insight: normalising by state land area changes the interpretation. A large state does not automatically appear important just because it has more land. The map instead highlights where protection covers a larger share of the state.

Chapter 2

Which Protected-Area Types Dominate?

The second part examines the structure of Australia's protected-area network. Here, "type" refers to categories of protected areas, such as Indigenous Protected Areas, national parks, nature reserves and conservation reserves. These categories contribute differently: some contain many small sites, while others cover very large areas.

4. Protected-Area Types Contribute Unequally

This labelled bar chart compares how much protected land each protected-area type contributes to the national network. A bar chart is used instead of a donut chart because it makes category comparison clearer and allows the exact percentage values to be labelled.

Key insight: Indigenous Protected Areas contribute the largest share of protected land by area. This shows that Australia's protected-area network is shaped not only by national parks, but also by Indigenous land management and other reserve categories.

5. The Largest Protected Areas Are Mostly Inland

This ranking shows the largest individual protected areas. It identifies the specific places that contribute most strongly to Australia's total protected-area hectares.

Key insight: the largest protected areas are mostly inland and remote. This explains why the national protected-area total can be strongly influenced by a relatively small number of very large sites.

6. Protection Has Expanded Over Time

The cumulative area chart shows when protected areas entered the network based on available gazettal years. The small chart below acts as an interactive time selector: drag across it to zoom into a specific period.

Key insight: protected-area growth does not increase evenly. The steep rises suggest periods when major new protected areas were added, rather than slow and constant growth over time. Use the lower mini chart to focus on specific decades and inspect when the largest jumps occurred.

Chapter 3

Where Do Protection Gaps Remain?

The final part connects protected-area distribution with conservation need. A large protected-area network does not mean that all species records or threatened ecological communities are equally covered. These charts compare protection coverage across species status, management categories and local protected-area density.

7. Species Protection Coverage Differs by Status

This slope chart compares the percentage of terrestrial and marine species records located inside protected areas. Each line represents one EPBC status category. The slope makes it easier to see whether marine or terrestrial records have higher protection coverage within the same threat category.

Key insight: protection coverage differs across both threat status and environment. Lines with steeper slopes show larger differences between terrestrial and marine species records, suggesting that protected-area coverage is not evenly distributed across environments.

8. IUCN Categories Have Different Management Roles

This bubble chart compares IUCN management categories using three measures at once. The horizontal axis shows the number of protected areas, the vertical axis shows total protected-area hectares, and bubble size represents the average area of one protected area in that category. This helps reveal whether a category contains many small sites or fewer very large sites.

Key insight: IUCN categories play different roles in the protected-area network. Category VI covers the largest total area, while categories such as Ia contain many more individual sites but smaller average area. This shows that management categories should not be compared by count alone; total area and average site size change the interpretation.

9. Threatened Ecological Communities Vary by State

This heatmap compares listed threatened ecological communities by state and EPBC status. Each cell represents one state-status combination, and darker colour indicates a higher number of listed ecological communities.

Key insight: threatened ecological communities are not evenly distributed across states. New South Wales stands out with high counts in severe categories, while other states show lower or more concentrated patterns. The heatmap makes the state-by-status structure easier to compare than stacked bars.

10. Protected Area Density Forms Local Clusters

This bin map groups protected-area locations into geographic grid cells. Darker cells contain more protected areas, but this measures site density rather than total protected hectares. It is useful for showing where many protected-area sites cluster together.

Key insight: south-eastern Australia and south-western Western Australia appear darker because they contain many small protected areas close together. Inland Australia often has fewer protected areas, but some are extremely large. This explains why Chart 1 and Chart 10 appear different: Chart 1 highlights protected-area size, while Chart 10 highlights local clustering and site density.

Conclusion

Australia Protects a Large Area, but Protection Is Uneven

Overall, Australia's protected-area network is extensive, but its distribution is uneven. Large inland reserves dominate total protected area, while coastal regions contain many smaller protected areas clustered close together. This means that area, density and location need to be interpreted together.

The visualisations also show that protected-area type matters. Indigenous Protected Areas contribute a major share of total protected land, while other categories contain many smaller sites or serve different management roles. At the same time, species protection coverage and threatened ecological communities vary across environments and states.

The key issue is therefore not only how much land is protected, but where protection is located, what type of protection it represents, and whether it aligns with conservation need. A strong protected-area network should be evaluated through both spatial distribution and ecological relevance.
